Why I Started This Blog

When I first took Management Science at Carnegie Mellon University with Professor Holly Wiberg, I honestly had no idea what the subject was about. I assumed it would be something similar to business management or organizational strategy. Instead, I found myself learning about linear programming, duality, network optimization, and algorithms such as the simplex method.

Professor Wiberg is an incredibly thoughtful teacher: clear, patient, and genuinely supportive of her students. Although I was able to follow the lectures and complete the assignments, I often felt that I was understanding how to solve the problems without fully understanding why everything worked.

A year later, I had the opportunity to serve as a teaching assistant for Optimization for Decision Making, taught by Professor Peter Caulkins. Teaching the material forced me to revisit many concepts that I had only partially understood as a student. I also began reading books such as Introduction to Mathematical Programming and other optimization references, gradually filling in the gaps that had been left during my first exposure to the subject.

This blog is the result of that journey.

Its primary purpose is surprisingly selfish: it is my own notebook. Writing is the best way I know to organize ideas and remember concepts. Every post is an attempt to explain something that I once found confusing.

At the same time, I hope these articles are useful for readers who have little, or even no, background in optimization. If you have not studied mathematics for years, or have always been intimidated by mathematical notation, I hope this series shows that optimization is far more intuitive than it first appears.

Two Ways to Learn Optimization

Through my admittedly limited experience, I think optimization can be understood from two complementary perspectives.

The first is problem formulation.

This is the art of translating a real-world problem into mathematics. What are the decision variables? What exactly are we trying to optimize? Which constraints represent reality, and which are simplifying assumptions? How do we justify the model, and what are its limitations?

To me, this is the most creative part of optimization.

Unlike many machine learning problems, where we use historical data to predict future outcomes, optimization often begins with a blank sheet of paper. We first need to build a mathematical representation of reality before any algorithm can solve it. A well-formulated model is often more valuable than a sophisticated algorithm applied to the wrong problem.

The second perspective is the mathematics behind the algorithms.

Once the model has been formulated, another fascinating question emerges: How do we actually solve it?

This leads to beautiful ideas from linear algebra, geometry, graph theory, and computer science. Topics such as the simplex method, interior-point methods, branch-and-bound, network flow algorithms, and dynamic programming are not just computational tricks. They each reveal a different way of thinking about optimization.

This blog will gradually explore both perspectives.

Is Optimization Actually Useful?

I certainly think so.

Many optimization models are already embedded in our daily lives. They determine how airlines schedule aircraft, how package delivery routes are planned, how electricity is dispatched across power grids, how hospitals allocate resources, and how bike-sharing systems rebalance bicycles throughout a city.

Sometimes I even catch myself wondering:

Could optimization be applied to dating or matchmaking?

Whether the answer is yes or no is less interesting than the habit of thinking that optimization develops. Once you begin learning it, you start seeing decision problems everywhere.

Why I Enjoy It

What I enjoy most is not necessarily finding the optimal answer, but the process of building a model that captures an incredibly complicated world.

Reality is messy.

People are irrational. Information is incomplete. Objectives conflict with one another. Every model is an approximation.

Optimization constantly asks the same question:

How can we build a mathematical model that is simple enough to solve, yet realistic enough to be useful?

I find that question endlessly fascinating.

Perhaps surprisingly, this interest has also led me toward psychology and philosophy. Human decisions rarely follow perfect mathematical rules, yet mathematical models often provide remarkably useful approximations. Understanding where those approximations succeed, and where they fail, is just as interesting as the mathematics itself.

Another pleasant surprise was discovering that the mathematics itself is actually enjoyable.

Before coming to Carnegie Mellon, I had not seriously studied advanced mathematics for many years. At first, linear algebra and optimization notation looked almost like another language. But as I slowly worked through the ideas, they became much more intuitive than I had expected.

If someone like me can eventually make sense of these concepts, I hope others can as well.

A Final Thought

Outside optimization, one of my favorite subjects is epistemology, the branch of philosophy that asks what knowledge is, how we know something is true, and how we distinguish good reasoning from bad reasoning.

In many ways, optimization feels connected to that pursuit.

Both disciplines ask us to start with assumptions, reason carefully, acknowledge limitations, and search for better answers rather than perfect ones.

Perhaps that is why I enjoy optimization so much.

It is not only about finding the best solution to a mathematical model.

It is also about learning how to think clearly in a world that is wonderfully, frustratingly, and beautifully complex.
