1. Product mix

Production Planning

Product mix determines how much of each product to produce or purchase in order to optimize an objective such as minimizing cost or maximizing profit. Decision variables typically represent quantities, while the model is constrained by factors such as production capacity, resource availability, demand, or budget.

1.1 Example of a Make or Buy Question

A steel manufacturer plans to transition part of its production to hydrogen-based Direct Reduced Iron (DRI) technology and requires 12,000 kg of hydrogen per day. The company already operates hydrogen production facilities and has established supply contracts with external vendors. The decision is therefore limited to determining the quantity of each hydrogen type to produce internally and purchase externally.

Three hydrogen pathways are available: green hydrogen, blue hydrogen, and grey hydrogen. The cost of producing hydrogen internally is $5.00/kg for green hydrogen, $3.50/kg for blue hydrogen, and $2.00/kg for grey hydrogen. Alternatively, hydrogen can be purchased from external suppliers at $5.50/kg, $4.00/kg, and $2.50/kg, respectively. The carbon intensities of green, blue, and grey hydrogen are 0.5, 3.0, and 9.0 kg CO₂ per kg H₂, respectively.

To support the company’s decarbonization goals, at least 10% of total hydrogen demand must be supplied by green hydrogen. Furthermore, the average carbon intensity of the hydrogen portfolio must not exceed 3.5 kg CO₂ per kg H₂. The company’s internal production facilities can supply at most 6,000 kg of hydrogen per day. External suppliers can provide at most 8,000 kg per day in total. In addition, supplier contracts limit purchases of green, blue, and grey hydrogen to 2,000 kg, 4,000 kg, and 5,000 kg per day, respectively.

Formulate a linear programming model to determine how much green, blue, and grey hydrogen should be produced internally and how much should be purchased externally in order to minimize total daily hydrogen procurement cost while satisfying all demand, environmental, production, and supply constraints.

1.2 LP formulation

Sets

Let

\[ H=\{G,B,R\} \]

denote the set of hydrogen types, where: \(G\) = Green Hydrogen, \(B\) = Blue Hydrogen, \(R\) = Grey Hydrogen

and

\[ S=\{M,P\} \]

denote the set of sourcing options, where \(M\) = Internal Production (Make) and \(P\) = External Procurement (Buy)

Parameters

The following parameters are known:

  • \(D=12000\): daily hydrogen demand (kg/day)
  • \(c_{i,s}\): cost of obtaining one kilogram of hydrogen type \(i\) through source \(s\)
  • \(e_i\): carbon intensity (kg CO\(_2\)/kg H\(_2\)) of hydrogen type \(i\)

The cost coefficients are:

Hydrogen Type Internal Production (\(M\)) External Purchase (\(P\))
Green (\(G\)) $5.00/kg $5.50/kg
Blue (\(B\)) $3.50/kg $4.00/kg
Grey (\(R\)) $2.00/kg $2.50/kg

The carbon intensity coefficients (\(e_i\))are:

\[ e_G=0.5,\qquad e_B=3.0,\qquad e_R=9.0. \]

Decision Variables

Let

\[ x_{i,s} = \text{kg of hydrogen type } i \text{ obtained through source } s \qquad i\in H,\; s\in S. \]

Objective Function

Minimize total daily hydrogen procurement cost:

\[ \min Z = \sum_{i\in H} \sum_{s\in S} c_{i,s}x_{i,s} \]

Constraints

Hydrogen demand must be satisfied

\[ \sum_{i\in H} \sum_{s\in S} x_{i,s} = D \]

Internal production capacity

\[ \sum_{i\in H} x_{i,M} \leq 6000 \]

External supplier capacity

\[ \sum_{i\in H} x_{i,P} \leq 8000 \]

At least 10% of total hydrogen must be green hydrogen

\[ \sum_{s\in S} x_{G,s} \geq 0.10D \]

Average carbon intensity must not exceed 3.5 kg CO\(_2\)/kg H\(_2\)

\[ \sum_{i\in H} e_i \left( \sum_{s\in S} x_{i,s} \right) \leq 3.5D \]

Supplier contract limits

\[ x_{G,P} \leq 2000 \]

\[ x_{B,P} \leq 4000 \]

\[ x_{R,P} \leq 5000 \]

Non-negativity

\[ x_{i,s} \geq 0, \qquad \forall i\in H,\; s\in S \]

Equivalent Formulation Without Sets and Indices

The same model can be written by explicitly listing every decision variable. Let

  • \(x_{G,M}\) = green hydrogen produced internally
  • \(x_{G,P}\) = green hydrogen purchased externally
  • \(x_{B,M}\) = blue hydrogen produced internally
  • \(x_{B,P}\) = blue hydrogen purchased externally
  • \(x_{R,M}\) = grey hydrogen produced internally
  • \(x_{R,P}\) = grey hydrogen purchased externally

The objective function becomes

\[ \min Z = 5x_{G,M} +3.5x_{B,M} +2x_{R,M} +5.5x_{G,P} +4x_{B,P} +2.5x_{R,P} \]

subject to

Hydrogen demand must be satisfied

\[ x_{G,M}+x_{G,P} +x_{B,M}+x_{B,P} +x_{R,M}+x_{R,P} = 12000 \]

Internal production capacity

\[ x_{G,M} +x_{B,M} +x_{R,M} \leq 6000 \]

External supplier capacity

\[ x_{G,P} +x_{B,P} +x_{R,P} \leq 8000 \]

At least 10% of total hydrogen must be green hydrogen

\[ x_{G,M} +x_{G,P} \geq 1200 \]

Average carbon intensity must not exceed 3.5 kg CO\(_2\)/kg H\(_2\)

\[ 0.5(x_{G,M}+x_{G,P}) + 3(x_{B,M}+x_{B,P}) + 9(x_{R,M}+x_{R,P}) \leq 42000 \]

Supplier contract limits

\[ x_{G,P}\leq 2000 \]

\[ x_{B,P}\leq 4000 \]

\[ x_{R,P}\leq 5000 \]

Non-negativity

\[ x_{G,M},x_{G,P}, x_{B,M},x_{B,P}, x_{R,M},x_{R,P} \geq 0 \]

Both formulations are mathematically identical. In this example there are only 6 decision variables, so writing them out individually is manageable. We can also use indexed formulation which scales naturally to larger problems.

The choice of notation is also flexible. We might define \(M_i\) and \(B_i\) to represent the quantities made and purchased. Some models may use superscripts, such as \(c_i^M\) and \(c_i^P\). These formulations are mathematically equivalent; the choice of notation is largely a matter of clarity, convenience, and the complexity of the problem being modeled.

2. Blending(Mixing) Problem

A production planning model treats each finished product as a single decision variable. It asks: “How many units of each product should we make?” The model does not care what is inside the product. It only tracks whether you have enough resources (e.g.,labor hours, machine time, raw materials, or budget) to produce it.

A blending problem goes one level deeper. It asks: “What should be inside the product, and in what proportions?”

Consider you have a secret party beverage menu where you blend beer, syrup, water, and sugar to produce several drinks. The production planning model would simply say: “Make 50 cups of Drink A and 30 cups of Drink B.” The blending model asks: “For each cup of Drink A, how much beer, syrup, water, and sugar goes in — and does the resulting mixture meet the alcohol and sweetness targets?”

The Defining Characteristic: Mass Balance

The heart of every blending problem is the mass balance principle. In a classical mixing problem:

\[\text{Total ingredients in} = \text{Total blended product out}\]

Every unit of every ingredient needs to be accounted for. Nothing appears from nowhere; nothing disappears. If you pour 200 liters of beer, 50 liters of syrup, and 150 liters of water into a vat, you get exactly 400 liters of blended drink.

This gives rise to the fundamental blending constraint:

\[\sum_{i} x_{ij} = q_j \quad \forall j\]

where \(x_{ij}\) is the quantity of ingredient \(i\) used in product \(j\), and \(q_j\) is the total quantity of product \(j\) produced.

But in practice, ingredients usually evaporate, spill, shrink, or get consumed in chemical reactions during processing. The mass balance still holds but it simply expands to include losses as an explicit term:

\[\text{Total ingredients in} = \text{Total blended product out} + \text{Losses}\]

In LP terms, a yield factor \(\lambda_i \in (0, 1]\) captures how much of ingredient \(i\) actually makes it into the final product. The blending constraint becomes:

\[\sum_{i} \lambda_i \, x_{ij} = q_j \quad \forall j\]

When \(\lambda_i = 1\), the ingredient transfers perfectly with no loss. When \(\lambda_i = 0.95\), 5% evaporates during mixing and only 95% of what you pour in ends up in the final blend.

The Second Characteristic: Quality Attributes

Beyond mass balance, each ingredient carries one or more quality attributes that propagate into the final mixture:

Quality attributes across blending domains
Domain Ingredients Quality Attribute
Fuel refining Crude streams Octane rating, sulfur content
Beverages Beer, syrup, water, sugar Alcohol %, sweetness, calories
Animal feed Corn, soy, wheat, minerals Protein %, fat %, fiber %
Steel production Scrap grades, alloys Carbon %, tensile strength
Paint mixing Pigments, solvents, binders Color, viscosity, drying time
Food processing Oils, fats, additives Saturated fat %, flavor index

Quality blends proportionally — a critical assumption. If Ingredient A has 8% alcohol and Ingredient B has 0% alcohol, a 50/50 blend yields exactly 4% alcohol. This linear mixing rule is what keeps the blending problem tractable as a linear program.

The quality constraint for a minimum threshold \(\theta\) on attribute \(\alpha\) takes the form:

\[\frac{\sum_{i} \alpha_i \, x_{ij}}{q_j} \geq \theta_j \quad \forall j\]

or equivalently in linearised form:

\[\sum_{i} \alpha_i \, x_{ij} \geq \theta_j \cdot q_j \quad \forall j\]

Side-by-Side Contrast

Structural comparison
Feature Production Planning Blending
Decision variable Units of finished product Quantity of each ingredient per product
Product composition Not tracked Explicitly modeled
Core constraint Resource capacity Mass balance + quality targets
Resource focus Labor, machines, budget Ingredient availability
Quality tracking None Octane, alcohol, sweetness, etc.
Math structure \(\sum_j a_{ij} x_j \leq b_i\) \(\sum_i x_{ij} = q_j\) and \(\sum_i \alpha_i x_{ij} \geq \theta \cdot q_j\)

2.1 Example of a Petroleum Blending Problem

A petroleum refinery produces three grades of gasoline: Regular, Mid-grade, and Premium. Each gasoline grade is created by blending three available components:

Component Available (L/day) Cost ($/L) Octane Rating
Reformate 4,000 0.95 98
Alkylate 3,000 1.10 102
Straight-run Gasoline 6,000 0.75 84

Each gasoline grade has a different selling price and minimum octane requirement.

Product Selling Price ($/L) Minimum Octane
Regular 1.20 87
Mid-grade 1.35 91
Premium 1.55 95

Assume that the daily market demand is at most:

  • Regular: 6,000 L
  • Mid-grade: 4,000 L
  • Premium: 3,000 L

How many liters of each gasoline grade should the refinery produce, and how should each blending component be allocated among the three products, in order to maximize daily profit?

Hint: The solution must satisfy the following constraints:

2.2 LP formulation

Sets

\[ I = \{\text{Reformate}, \text{Alkylate}, \text{Straight-run Gasoline}\} \]

\[ J = \{\text{Regular}, \text{Mid-grade}, \text{Premium}\} \]

Parameters

Parameter Meaning
\(a_i\) Available supply of component \(i\)
\(c_i\) Cost per liter of component \(i\)
\(o_i\) Octane rating of component \(i\)
\(p_j\) Selling price per liter of product \(j\)
\(r_j\) Minimum octane requirement of product \(j\)
\(d_j\) Maximum market demand for product \(j\)

Decision Variables

\[ x_{ij} = \text{liters of component } i \text{ blended into product } j \]

where \(i \in I\) and \(j \in J\).

Objective Function

\[ \max Z = \sum_{j \in J} p_j \left(\sum_{i \in I} x_{ij}\right) - \sum_{i \in I}\sum_{j \in J} c_i x_{ij} \]

Constraints

Component availability:

\[ \sum_{j \in J} x_{ij} \le a_i \quad \forall i \in I \]

Market demand:

\[ \sum_{i \in I} x_{ij} \le d_j \quad \forall j \in J \]

Octane requirement:

\[ \sum_{i \in I} o_i x_{ij} \ge r_j \sum_{i \in I} x_{ij} \quad \forall j \in J \]

Non-negativity:

\[ x_{ij} \ge 0 \quad \forall i \in I,\ j \in J \]

Equivalent Formulation Without Sets and Indices

Decision Variables

Let

  • \(R_R\) = liters of Reformate blended into Regular

  • \(A_R\) = liters of Alkylate blended into Regular

  • \(S_R\) = liters of Straight-run Gasoline blended into Regular

  • \(R_M\) = liters of Reformate blended into Mid-grade

  • \(A_M\) = liters of Alkylate blended into Mid-grade

  • \(S_M\) = liters of Straight-run Gasoline blended into Mid-grade

  • \(R_P\) = liters of Reformate blended into Premium

  • \(A_P\) = liters of Alkylate blended into Premium

  • \(S_P\) = liters of Straight-run Gasoline blended into Premium

Objective Function

Maximize daily profit.

\[ \begin{aligned} \max Z =\;& 1.20(R_R+A_R+S_R) \\ &+1.35(R_M+A_M+S_M) \\ &+1.55(R_P+A_P+S_P) \\ &-0.95(R_R+R_M+R_P) \\ &-1.10(A_R+A_M+A_P) \\ &-0.75(S_R+S_M+S_P) \end{aligned} \]

Constraints

Component Availability

\[ R_R+R_M+R_P \le 4000 \]

\[ A_R+A_M+A_P \le 3000 \]

\[ S_R+S_M+S_P \le 6000 \]

Market Demand

\[ R_R+A_R+S_R \le 6000 \]

\[ R_M+A_M+S_M \le 4000 \]

\[ R_P+A_P+S_P \le 3000 \]

Minimum Octane Rating

Regular gasoline:

\[ 98R_R+102A_R+84S_R \ge 87(R_R+A_R+S_R) \]

Mid-grade gasoline:

\[ 98R_M+102A_M+84S_M \ge 91(R_M+A_M+S_M) \]

Premium gasoline:

\[ 98R_P+102A_P+84S_P \ge 95(R_P+A_P+S_P) \]

Non-negativity

All decision variables are greater than or equal to zero.
